First Memories of Chess

My first memory of a chessboard dates back to the 1950s when my father, the Norwegian magician Ramiras, took me to the Bethania assembly hall in Stavanger. I was only about five years old and could barely reach the table.

During one game a crowd gathered around the board. A collective sigh went through the audience when my adult opponent lifted a bishop from b7 and maneuvered through the pieces before finally crushing my rook on h1. Fortunately, this early setback did not discourage me.

As the years passed I became an active player in the local school chess environment and looked up to several older players, among them future Nordic Junior Champion Thor Geir Harestad.

Meeting the Heroes

After my school chess years ended, I joined Stavanger Chess Club, where many of the city's strongest players gathered in the traditional, smoke-filled premises of the Stavanger Marine Engineers Association.

Progress was difficult. Promotion to the Master Class was restricted to a single player each year. After several intense qualification matches I finally succeeded and eventually achieved second place, behind Falkeid but ahead of the legendary Sverre Halvorsen.

Before leaving for military service, Sverre Halvorsen suggested that I should try correspondence chess. At first I hesitated because the path through the classes seemed long and demanding. Eventually I took a chance and entered directly into Master Class B, arguing that I had actually defeated Halvorsen in over-the-board chess.

Apparently that was considered a convincing argument.

The Search for the Grandmaster Title

After a long break that included over-the-board chess and bridge, I returned to correspondence chess in the late 1980s.

Roald Berthelsen became an important source of inspiration as I began the long journey towards the ICCF Grandmaster title. There were many hard tournaments and countless hours of analysis, but eventually the effort paid off.

One particularly enjoyable achievement was winning the Norwegian Team Championship together with Randaberg Chess Club, an accomplishment that even brought official recognition from the local mayor.

Electronic Mail Arrives

As technology developed, chess moves increasingly began to travel by e-mail rather than by traditional post. This was unfortunate for those who loved collecting stamps, but it also transformed correspondence chess forever.

The transition was not without controversy. Players and organisers debated how thinking times should be calculated, how tournaments should be administered, and how to deal with communications that crossed time zones and international date lines.

Sending a move to countries with limited internet access could still be a challenge, and many practical issues had to be solved before the new system became universally accepted.

Discussion Forums and the Online Community

The Correspondence Chess Message Board (TCCMB), founded by John Knudsen in the United States, became one of the most active correspondence chess forums of the 1990s. It attracted passionate discussions and introduced me to many good friends around the world.

Inspired by friends in the Norwegian correspondence chess community, I started a Norwegian discussion forum on 29 November 2005 while sitting in a rainy hotel reception on Madeira with access to a public computer.

The forum eventually attracted hundreds of registered users and became a lively meeting place where players discussed everything from Olympiads and opening theory to server chess, tablebases and time-control rules.

New Times

Gradually the traditional postcard era came to an end. Internet-based servers took over and provided a faster and more reliable playing environment than ever before.

The transformation from stamps and postcards to e-mail and modern servers marked the beginning of a new era in correspondence chess. It changed how games were played, how players communicated and how the chess community interacted across national borders.

Yet the spirit of correspondence chess remained unchanged: patience, analysis, creativity and a shared passion for the game.
